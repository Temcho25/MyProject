Xi'an is the city most people have heard of for one reason — the Terracotta Army — and then end up loving for a dozen others. It has a complete Ming-era city wall you can cycle on, a night-time food street that smells of cumin and grilled lamb, and Buddhist pagodas older than most European cathedrals. This guide covers what to see, how to get there, how many days to plan and the small details that make a first visit easier.

## Where Xi'an is and why it matters

Xi'an is the capital of Shaanxi province in north-western China, roughly in the geographical middle of the country. Under its older name, Chang'an, it served as the capital of several Chinese dynasties, including the Han and the Tang — the period many Chinese people still think of as the high point of their civilisation.

Chang'an was also the eastern starting point of the Silk Road. In 2014 UNESCO inscribed the "Silk Roads: the Routes Network of Chang'an–Tianshan Corridor" as a World Heritage Site, shared by China, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. For travellers from the Caucasus and Central Asia this gives Xi'an a special feeling: it is the other end of a road that once passed close to home.

## The Terracotta Army

### How it was discovered

In March 1974, farmers digging a well in Lintong District, east of Xi'an, struck fragments of clay figures. They had found part of the burial complex of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor to unify China in the 3rd century BC. The Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor, including the warrior pits, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1987.

### Pits 1, 2 and 3

The site is organised around three excavated pits, each in its own hall:

- **Pit 1** — the largest and the one you have seen in photos: long rows of infantry facing east, with restoration work visible at the back.
- **Pit 2** — a mixed force of archers, cavalry and chariots; much of it is still only partly excavated, which is interesting in itself.
- **Pit 3** — the smallest, usually interpreted as the command post.

Our tip: visit Pit 3 and Pit 2 first and finish at Pit 1. Ending with the biggest hall makes the visit build up rather than peak in the first ten minutes. Leave time for the exhibition hall too — it usually displays the bronze chariots and individual figures up close, so you can see the different faces, hairstyles and armour.

### How far from the city

The museum is roughly 40 km east of central Xi'an. Allow at least an hour each way by road, more in traffic. A common public-transport route is Metro Line 9 to Huaqingchi station and then a short tourist bus ride; bus numbers and routes change, so check on the day. Plan half a day for the visit itself — three to four hours on site is comfortable.

> **Important:** Tickets are sold with real-name registration and can sell out on busy days, especially during Chinese public holidays. Book in advance with the passport you will carry, and check current opening hours and prices on the official channels before your trip.

## Inside the city

### City Wall: cycling on top

Xi'an's City Wall is about 13.7 km around and is one of the best-preserved city walls in China. You can walk a section, but the classic experience is renting a bicycle on top and riding the full loop. Rental points are near the main gates, the South Gate being the most popular starting point. A full loop takes most people around two hours with photo stops. Prices, deposit rules and rental time limits change, so check them at the ticket office.

Late afternoon is the best time: softer light and, if you time it right, the lanterns coming on along the wall.

### Bell Tower and Drum Tower

The Bell Tower stands in the very centre of the walled city, at the crossing of the four main streets; the current building dates to 1384, the early Ming dynasty. The Drum Tower is a short walk away. In the past the bell marked the morning and the drum the evening. Both are beautiful when illuminated after dark.

### Muslim Quarter food street

Right behind the Drum Tower begins the Muslim Quarter (Huimin Jie), home to Xi'an's Hui Muslim community for centuries. In the evening it becomes one long food street: lamb skewers, flatbreads, persimmon cakes, sesame sweets, fresh pomegranate juice in season. It is crowded and touristy on the main lane — step into the side streets for a calmer feel.

### Giant Wild Goose Pagoda

South of the wall stands the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, built in 652 under the Tang dynasty to house the Buddhist scriptures that the monk Xuanzang brought back from India. The square in front of it hosts a large musical fountain in the evenings (check the schedule locally).

## How many days do you need?

- **1 full day** — Terracotta Army in the morning, City Wall and Muslim Quarter in the evening. Doable but rushed.
- **2 days** — the comfortable minimum: one day for the warriors, one for the city.
- **3 days** — adds the Shaanxi History Museum (reservations are usually needed), a slower pace and evenings for food.

## Best season to visit

Spring (around April–May) and autumn (around September–October) usually bring the most comfortable weather. Summers are hot, and winters are cold and dry but less crowded — the Terracotta Army in winter, with fewer tour groups, can be a pleasure. Avoid Chinese national holidays (especially the early-October Golden Week) if you dislike crowds.

## How to get to Xi'an

**By high-speed train.** Xi'an North is one of China's major rail hubs, with fast connections to Beijing, Luoyang, Zhengzhou, Chengdu and many other cities. For first-time visitors trains are often more convenient than flights: no airport transfers and city-centre-to-city-centre travel. See our [guide to China's high-speed trains](/en/blog/china-high-speed-trains-guide/).

**By plane.** Xi'an Xianyang International Airport is north-west of the city. Metro Line 14 links the airport with Xi'an North Railway Station, from where you can change into the city.

## What to eat in Xi'an

Xi'an is a noodle-and-bread city, very different from Cantonese or Sichuan cooking:

- **Roujiamo** — often called the "Chinese burger": chopped stewed meat in a crisp flatbread. In the Muslim Quarter it is made with beef or lamb.
- **Biangbiang noodles** — wide, hand-pulled belt noodles with chilli oil, garlic and vinegar. The Chinese character "biang" is famous for being one of the most complicated to write.
- **Yangrou paomo** — lamb soup into which you break flatbread by hand; the smaller the pieces, the better the bread absorbs the broth.
- **Liangpi** — cold, chewy noodles with a tangy sauce, perfect on a warm day.

## Practical tips

- **Carry your passport.** It is needed for museum tickets, trains and hotels.
- **Start early at the Terracotta Army.** The halls fill up quickly by late morning.
- **Book a guide or an audio guide.** Without context, Pit 1 is impressive; with context, it is unforgettable.
- **Wear comfortable shoes.** Between the museum, the wall and the food street, you will walk a lot.
- **Set up mobile payments before you arrive.** Many food stalls prefer them; keep some cash as a backup.

## FAQ

### Can I visit the Terracotta Army on my own?

Yes. Many travellers go by metro and tourist bus or by taxi. The main challenges are booking tickets in advance and understanding what you see — that is where a guide makes the biggest difference.

### Is one day in Xi'an enough?

It is enough to see the Terracotta Army and walk the centre, but two days let you enjoy the city wall and the food without rushing.

### Can you take photos inside the pits?

Photography without flash is generally allowed in the main halls; rules for special exhibitions can differ, so follow the signs.

### What is the best time of day to visit the warriors?

As close to opening as possible. Mornings are quieter, and you can spend the evening on the City Wall and in the Muslim Quarter.
