China rewards first-time visitors with ancient walls, futuristic cities, painted-looking mountains and superb food. It also works differently from most countries. Familiar apps don't open, people pay with their phones, and many signs are only in Chinese.

A little preparation goes a long way. This guide covers what first-time travellers ask us most, in the order you'll need it.

## Documents and entry rules

### Visa or visa-free? It depends on your passport

China's entry rules depend entirely on your citizenship, and they have changed often in recent years. China has steadily expanded visa-free entry: many European countries, as well as the UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and others, can currently visit for up to 30 days without a visa under a unilateral policy. Under the latest official notices we have seen, that policy runs until 31 December 2026, so if your trip falls in 2027, check whether it has been extended.

There is also a **240-hour (10-day) visa-free transit** option for citizens of a long list of countries (57 as of August 2026). It applies when you fly *through* China to a third country with a confirmed onward ticket, and you must stay within designated regions and enter through designated ports.

### Citizens of Georgia

Georgia and China have a mutual visa exemption agreement that came into force on 28 May 2024. Holders of ordinary Georgian passports can stay in China without a visa for up to **30 days per entry**, and no more than **90 days in any 180-day period**. Longer stays, and purposes such as work or study, still require a visa.

### Citizens of Russia

Since 15 September 2025, Russian citizens with ordinary passports can enter China without a visa for up to **30 days** for tourism, business, visiting family and friends, exchanges or transit. In May 2026 China announced that this arrangement is extended until **31 December 2027**.

> **Important:** Everything in this section is accurate as of November 2026, but entry rules can change with little notice. Always confirm the current rules with the Chinese embassy or consulate in your country, or with your airline, before you book. No travel company, including us, can guarantee entry: the final decision is always made by the border officer.

### Passport tips

- Make sure your passport has plenty of validity left; six months beyond your entry date is the safe standard.
- Keep blank pages for stamps.
- Carry your passport every day. You need it for hotels, train tickets, and some museums and attractions.
- Save a photo or scan of your passport somewhere you can reach offline.

## Internet: what works and what doesn't

Many Western services are blocked in mainland China, including Google (Search, Gmail, Maps), WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, YouTube and many news sites. Plan for this before you land, not after.

Your main options:

- **International roaming.** Roaming data from a foreign SIM card is routed through your home operator, so your usual apps typically keep working. It can be expensive, so check your tariff.
- **Travel eSIM.** Many providers sell China or Asia data packages that you install before departure. Choose one that clearly states it supports the services you rely on, and read recent reviews.
- **Local SIM card.** Cheap and fast, but registration requires your passport, and blocked services stay blocked.

Before you fly, agree with family on an app that works in China (usually WeChat) and download offline maps and translation packs.

## Money: Alipay, WeChat Pay and cash

China is one of the most cashless countries in the world. Street vendors, taxis and small cafés usually expect a QR-code payment.

The good news: both **Alipay** and **WeChat Pay** now allow foreign visitors to link international Visa and Mastercard cards (other networks vary) after passport verification. Set this up at home and make a small test payment if you can.

A few practical notes:

- Small payments (historically up to 200 yuan) are usually free; larger ones can carry a service fee of about 3%, and your bank may add its own foreign transaction fee. Check the current terms in the app.
- Install both apps: some merchants accept only one.
- **Carry some cash anyway.** Phones die, cards get blocked by over-cautious banks, and some rural places still prefer notes. Withdraw yuan from bank ATMs in China or exchange money at home.
- Tell your bank you are travelling to China so it doesn't flag your payments.

## Essential apps to install before you go

You don't need dozens of apps. Cover these categories:

- **Messaging:** WeChat. It is also how local guides, hotels and new friends will contact you.
- **Maps:** Amap (Gaode) or Baidu Maps are the most detailed; Apple Maps also works in China. Google Maps is blocked and often inaccurate there.
- **Translation:** an app with offline packs and camera translation for menus and signs (for example Microsoft Translator, Apple Translate or Baidu Translate).
- **Ride-hailing:** DiDi, available in English and also accessible inside Alipay.
- **Trains and flights:** the official 12306 railway app (it has an English version) or Trip.com.

## The language barrier

Outside hotels and big tourist sites, English is limited. Younger people in large cities often speak some, but don't count on it in taxis, restaurants or smaller towns.

What helps:

- Save your hotel's name and address **in Chinese characters** to show taxi drivers.
- Use camera translation for menus.
- Learn a few words: *nǐ hǎo* (hello), *xièxie* (thank you), *duōshǎo qián* (how much).

## Getting around: trains and flights

China's distances are big, but its transport is excellent.

![High-speed train at a modern Chinese railway station](photo:high-speed-train)

**High-speed rail** is often the best way to travel between cities up to around 1,000 km apart: stations are central, security is quicker than at airports, and the views are great. Tickets are tied to your passport, so book with the exact name and number. Popular routes sell out around holidays. Our [guide to China's high-speed trains](/en/blog/china-high-speed-trains-guide/) explains booking, stations and what to expect on board.

**Domestic flights** make sense for longer jumps, for example from Beijing to the south-west. Leave extra time for security, and remember that power banks have strict rules on board.

## Hotels and passport registration

Foreign visitors must register their place of stay with the police. When you stay in a **hotel**, the hotel does this for you at check-in using your passport. That's why reception will scan it.

Two things to know:

- Not every budget hotel is licensed to accept foreign guests. Book through international platforms or check the listing to avoid surprises.
- If you stay in a private home or apartment, registration is your (or your host's) responsibility, normally within 24 hours of arrival. Online registration has been introduced, but check the current procedure locally.

## Food and dietary notes

Chinese food is regional and incredibly varied: from Sichuan's numbing spice to delicate Cantonese dim sum, Xi'an noodles and Hunan chilli dishes.

![Street food stalls with snacks in China](photo:street-food)

- **Spice:** say *bù là* (not spicy) or *wēi là* (mildly spicy).
- **Vegetarian:** possible but tricky, because meat stock and lard are common. Carry a written note in Chinese.
- **Allergies:** peanuts, sesame and seafood are widespread. A translated allergy card is essential.
- **Tap water:** don't drink it. Bottled water is everywhere, and hotels provide kettles.
- **Halal:** look for restaurants with Arabic script or the 清真 sign, especially in cities like Xi'an.

## Etiquette in brief

- Don't stick chopsticks upright in rice; it is associated with funerals.
- Tipping isn't customary in most restaurants or taxis.
- Ask before photographing people, especially monks and older people.
- Avoid political discussions with strangers, and respect rules at temples and museums.

## Safety

China is generally considered safe for travellers, with low levels of violent crime. The usual precautions still apply: watch your belongings in crowds, use official taxis or ride-hailing apps, and be wary of friendly strangers inviting you to a "tea ceremony" or art gallery, which is a known tourist scam. Get travel insurance that covers medical care.

## Packing checklist

- Passport, printed bookings and insurance documents
- Phone with eSIM or roaming set up and payment apps ready
- Power bank (check airline limits) and a universal adapter
- Comfortable walking shoes: you will walk a lot, including steps on mountains and walls
- Layers: northern winters are very cold, and southern summers are hot and humid
- Basic medicines, tissues and hand sanitiser (public toilets often lack paper)

## FAQ

### Do I need a visa for my first trip to China?

It depends on your citizenship. Many countries currently have 30-day visa-free entry, and Georgian and Russian citizens with ordinary passports can also visit visa-free for up to 30 days under bilateral arrangements (as of November 2026). Always confirm with the Chinese embassy before booking.

### Can I use my foreign bank card in China?

Not always directly in shops, but you can link Visa or Mastercard cards to Alipay and WeChat Pay and pay by QR code almost anywhere. Bring some cash as a backup.

### Will WhatsApp and Google work in China?

Not on a local Chinese SIM or hotel Wi-Fi. International roaming and some travel eSIMs usually let you keep using them. Install WeChat before you go.
