Every winter, Harbin, the capital of Heilongjiang province in China's far northeast, turns into a city of ice. Whole buildings are carved from blocks cut out of the frozen Songhua River, lit from inside in every colour, and thousands of people walk between them in temperatures most travellers have never felt. If you have seen photos of glowing ice castles in China, they were almost certainly taken here.

This guide covers what the festival actually is, when it usually runs, which venues are worth your time, how cold it really gets, and how to fit Harbin into a wider trip to China.

## What the Harbin Ice and Snow Festival is

The China Harbin International Ice and Snow Festival is not a single park but a citywide season of events. It has been held since 1985, and the edition that opened in January 2026 was the 42nd. The festival combines several separate venues, each with its own ticket and character:

- **Harbin Ice and Snow World** – the largest venue and the one in most photographs: full-size ice buildings, slides and light shows.
- **Sun Island Snow Sculpture Expo** – huge white sculptures carved from compacted snow, best seen in daylight.
- **Zhaolin Park** – the traditional ice lantern show in the city centre, smaller and more intimate.

On top of that, the city itself is part of the experience: Central Street with its early 20th-century facades, the onion domes of St. Sophia Cathedral and the frozen Songhua River.

## When the festival runs

The season follows the cold. In recent years, Ice and Snow World has typically opened to visitors in the second half of December, the official opening ceremony of the festival has traditionally taken place on 5 January, and the main venues have stayed open until roughly late February, depending on the weather and the state of the ice.

> **Important:** exact dates are announced by the organisers each year, often only a few weeks before opening. Before you book flights, check the current year's opening and closing dates — the schedule depends on temperatures and can shift.

**Best time to go:** from early January to early February the sculptures are complete, the ice is clear and the cold is reliable. Late December can mean fewer crowds but some sculptures may still be under construction. Chinese New Year (late January or February, depending on the year) brings domestic holiday crowds and higher hotel prices.

## What to see in Harbin

![Snow sculpture at Sun Island in Harbin](photo:harbin-snow-sculpture)

### Harbin Ice and Snow World

This is the headline venue: a park on the north bank of the Songhua River where ice towers, temples, castles and slides are built anew each season. The secret is timing. Arrive in the mid-afternoon to see the ice in daylight, then stay until dark when the lights come on — that is when the park looks like the photos. Plan at least three to four hours, and take breaks in the heated pavilions.

### Sun Island Snow Sculpture Expo

Sun Island lies across the river from the old city. Here the material is snow rather than ice: enormous, detailed sculptures that look best in sunshine, when shadows bring out their texture. Go in the morning or early afternoon.

### Zhaolin Park

A city park close to Central Street with a long tradition of ice lantern displays. It is smaller and calmer than Ice and Snow World and works well as an evening walk if you are staying nearby.

### Central Street (Zhongyang Dajie)

A long pedestrian street paved with cobblestones, lined with European-style buildings from the time when Harbin grew around the Chinese Eastern Railway. It is the best place to feel the city's mixed Chinese and Russian heritage — and it leads straight to the river.

### St. Sophia Cathedral

The former Russian Orthodox cathedral with a green dome is Harbin's best-known landmark. It no longer functions as a church; the building is used as an exhibition space about the city's architecture. It is especially photogenic in the evening when it is lit up.

### The Songhua River in winter

In deep winter the river freezes solid, and parts of it are used for winter activities such as sledding and ice slides. Use only organised, marked areas — never walk out onto ice on your own.

## How cold it gets and how to dress

Harbin is one of the coldest large cities in China. In January, average daytime highs sit around −12 to −13°C and nights commonly fall to around −22 to −25°C; on some days it gets colder still. The air is usually dry, which makes it more bearable than it sounds, but long hours outside at night demand serious preparation.

**What to pack:**

- A long down jacket with a hood (a thigh-length coat is far warmer than a short one)
- Thermal base layers for top and bottom, plus a fleece or wool mid-layer
- Insulated, waterproof winter boots with a thick sole and room for warm socks
- Wool socks (take spares), a warm hat covering the ears, a scarf or neck gaiter
- Two pairs of gloves: thin touchscreen gloves inside, warm mittens outside
- Disposable heat packs — widely sold in China, and very useful in boots and pockets

**Tip:** phone batteries drain fast in the cold. Keep your phone in an inside pocket and carry a power bank close to your body.

## How long to stay

Two full days in Harbin is a comfortable minimum: one for Ice and Snow World (afternoon into night) and one for Sun Island, Central Street, St. Sophia and the river. A third day gives you breathing room for bad weather, a slower pace or a trip out of town. ## How to get to Harbin

- **By air:** Harbin Taiping International Airport has domestic connections from most major Chinese cities, plus a number of international routes. From abroad, the trip usually involves a connection in Beijing, Shanghai or another hub.
- **By high-speed rail from Beijing:** the high-speed line links the two cities in roughly five hours on the fastest trains, mostly from Beijing Chaoyang station to Harbin West. Timetables change, so check the current schedule.

Trains and flights fill up during the festival and around Chinese New Year, so book early.

## What to eat in Harbin

Harbin's food is hearty, northern and shaped by its Russian past.

- **Guobaorou** – crispy pork slices in a sweet-and-sour glaze; Harbin is considered its home.
- **Harbin red sausage (hongchang)** – a smoked, garlicky sausage with Eastern European roots, sold all over the city.
- **Dalieba** – large round loaves of Russian-style bread, a popular souvenir.
- **Ice cream on Central Street** – eating an ice lolly at −20°C is a local ritual.
- **Hot pot and dumplings** – the perfect way to thaw after an evening at the ice park.

## Practical tips

- Set up mobile payments (Alipay or WeChat Pay with a foreign card) before you travel, and carry some cash as backup.
- Bring your passport every day: it is needed for train tickets, hotels and some attraction bookings.
- Venue tickets and opening hours change each season — check them shortly before your visit.
- Go to Ice and Snow World on a weekday if possible, and avoid the first evenings after the opening ceremony.
- Protect your camera: let it warm up slowly in a bag when you come inside to avoid condensation.

## FAQ

### When is the Harbin Ice Festival held?

Usually from late December to late February, with the official opening ceremony traditionally on 5 January. Exact dates are announced every year, so confirm them before booking.

### Is Harbin too cold for a first trip to China?

Not if you dress properly. With layers, good boots and heat packs, most people enjoy several hours outdoors, alternating with warm cafés and heated pavilions.

### Day or night — when should I visit Ice and Snow World?

Both: arrive in the afternoon and stay after sunset. The lighting is what makes the park unique.

### Can I combine Harbin with other cities?

Yes. Beijing is the most natural combination thanks to the high-speed rail link, and Xi'an is easy to add by domestic flight or train.
